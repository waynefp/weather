Formation Science

Painting the Polar Sky

The Sun continuously streams charged particles — electrons and protons — into space as the solar wind, travelling at 400–800 km/s. Earth's magnetosphere deflects most of this flow, but near the magnetic poles, field lines converge and funnel particles downward into the upper atmosphere along oval-shaped auroral zones.

When these energetic electrons collide with oxygen atoms at around 100–200 km altitude, they excite the atoms to higher energy states. As the oxygen returns to its ground state, it releases photons. At ~100 km, green (557.7 nm) is produced — the most common auroral colour. At higher altitudes above 200 km, excited oxygen emits rare red aurora. Below 100 km, nitrogen molecules produce blue and purple hues.

Aurora brightness and extent are governed by solar activity. Coronal mass ejections (CMEs) — massive bursts of solar plasma — can trigger geomagnetic storms when they strike Earth's magnetosphere, driving displays visible as far south as 45° latitude or lower during extreme events.

The Southern Hemisphere equivalent, Aurora Australis, mirrors the Borealis identically, with displays over Antarctica, southern New Zealand, and Patagonia.

Science & History

The Kp Index & Great Events

The Kp Index

A scale from 0–9 measuring global geomagnetic activity. Kp 5 = minor storm; Kp 7 = strong storm; Kp 9 = extreme. Visible aurora typically begins around Kp 4–5 for latitudes like Scotland or Canada.

The Carrington Event (1859)

The most powerful solar storm ever recorded. Aurora was seen as far south as Cuba and Hawaii. Telegraph systems failed globally — some operators reported sparks and paper fires from the induced currents.

Color by Altitude

Green: oxygen at ~100 km. Red: oxygen above 200 km (rare). Blue/purple: nitrogen below 100 km. Pink fringe: mix of nitrogen blue and oxygen green at the lower border.

Impact on Modern Infrastructure

When Beauty Becomes Disruption

Power Grid Disruption

Geomagnetically induced currents (GICs) flow through long power transmission lines during storms, saturating transformers. The 1989 Quebec blackout left 6 million people without power for 9 hours after a Kp 9 storm. A Carrington-level event today could disable large portions of national grids for weeks.

GPS & Radio Interference

Solar storms expand and perturb the ionosphere, bending and delaying GPS signals. During the May 2024 Kp 9 storm, aviation GPS systems in North America reported significant degradation. HF radio blackouts are common on the sunlit side of Earth during solar flares.

Pipeline Corrosion

GICs induced in long metal pipelines (Alaska Pipeline, Trans-Siberian lines) accelerate electrochemical corrosion. Engineers monitor geomagnetic activity and apply cathodic protection systems. Operators adjust operating pressures during severe storms to reduce stress on pipe welds.
