Formation Science

When Air Touches Its Dew Point

Fog is simply a cloud with its base at or near the Earth's surface — the same physics, just at ground level. It forms whenever air is cooled below its dew point temperature, the temperature at which the air can no longer hold all its water vapour as gas. The excess condenses into billions of tiny liquid droplets, each 1–100 micrometres in diameter, suspended in still or slowly moving air.

Radiation fog is the most common type. On clear, calm nights, the ground loses heat rapidly to space through longwave radiation. The lowest layers of air cool conductively. If surface temperature drops to the dew point, fog forms — often starting just before dawn when cooling reaches its maximum. It burns off as the sun heats the surface the following morning.

Advection fog forms when warm, moist air moves horizontally over a cold surface — the classic San Francisco Bay fog that rolls in from the Pacific over the cold California Current. It can persist day and night, driven by onshore winds rather than radiative cooling.

Valley fog forms when cold, dense air drains off surrounding hillsides and pools in low-lying terrain on clear nights. California's Tule Fog — the densest and most dangerous fog in the US — occurs in the Central Valley's "bowl" geometry, sometimes persisting for days during winter inversions with near-zero visibility over hundreds of kilometres.

Classification

Fog, Mist & Haze

Radiation Fog

Nocturnal cooling of land surface. Disperses rapidly after sunrise. Common in temperate regions on clear, calm nights.

Advection Fog

Warm air over cold sea surface. Can persist 24/7. Responsible for San Francisco's iconic fogscape and Grand Banks maritime fog (thickest on Earth).

Freezing Fog & Ice Fog

Supercooled droplets coat surfaces in rime ice. Ice fog (below –30°C) is composed of ice crystals — common in Arctic and Siberian cities.

Fog vs Mist vs Haze

Fog: visibility <1 km (liquid droplets). Mist: 1–2 km visibility (droplets). Haze: visibility reduced by dry particles (dust, smoke, pollution) — no water droplets required.

Impact

Aviation, Shipping, Roads & the Forest

Aviation & Shipping

Fog is responsible for more aviation delays and diversions than any other weather phenomenon. Category III instrument landing systems allow aircraft to land in near-zero visibility. At sea, the Grand Banks fog between Newfoundland and the Gulf Stream has claimed hundreds of ships — a key driver behind mandatory foghorns and later radar.

Road Accidents

Fog contributes to thousands of road fatalities annually. California's Tule Fog has triggered pile-ups involving 100+ vehicles on Highway 99. The UK's M25 and Germany's Autobahn experience regular fog-related multi-vehicle accidents. Variable message signs and automatic braking systems have reduced — but not eliminated — the risk.

Agriculture & Ecosystems

Fog drip — where droplets collect on leaf surfaces and drip to the ground — delivers up to 40% of the annual moisture budget for California coastal redwoods and Canary Island laurel forests. Conversely, persistent advection fog can suppress solar radiation and stunt crop growth. The Atacama coastal fog ("camanchaca") supports unique desert ecosystems in one of the driest places on Earth.
