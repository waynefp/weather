Formation Science

The Tropical Heat Engine

Hurricanes (called typhoons in the western Pacific and cyclones in the Indian Ocean) are powered entirely by the latent heat stored in warm tropical ocean water. When sea surface temperatures exceed 26°C to a depth of at least 50 metres, intense evaporation loads the atmosphere with moisture.

As this warm, humid air rises, it cools, releasing enormous amounts of latent heat through condensation into towering thunderstorm clusters called a tropical disturbance. The Earth's Coriolis effect imparts rotation — counterclockwise in the Northern Hemisphere, clockwise in the Southern — spinning the convective cluster into an organised tropical depression.

As winds strengthen and deep convection organises around a central core, the system passes through tropical storm intensity. The eye forms when strong upper-level outflow creates a descending column of clear, warm, calm air at the centre. The surrounding eyewall — a ring of intense thunderstorm convection — contains the storm's most violent winds and heaviest rainfall.

Rapid intensification — a wind speed increase of 56 km/h or more in 24 hours — occurs when all conditions align: warm deep water, low wind shear, and high humidity. Hurricane Patricia (2015) deepened from Category 1 to Category 5 in just 24 hours, reaching 345 km/h winds.

The Naming System

The World Meteorological Organisation maintains rotating lists of names for 6 basins. Atlantic names alternate male/female alphabetically. Retired names (Katrina, Maria, Harvey) were too deadly or costly to reuse.

Rapid Intensification

Increasingly common as ocean heat content rises. The window between a "manageable" storm forecast and a catastrophic one can be less than 24 hours — a critical challenge for evacuation planners.

Notable Events

Storms That Changed Policy and History

Hurricane Katrina — 2005

Category 3 at landfall near New Orleans, but with a catastrophic storm surge of up to 8.5 m. Levee failures flooded 80% of the city. 1,833 fatalities, $125 billion in damages. Exposed systemic failures in emergency preparedness and infrastructure maintenance.

Hurricane Harvey — 2017

Stalled over Houston for four days, dropping a record 1,538 mm of rain — the most from any US tropical cyclone. Catastrophic flooding across Harris County. 68 direct fatalities, $125 billion in damages. Highlighted the flooding risk of slow-moving storms.

Hurricane Maria — 2017

Category 4 devastated Puerto Rico, destroying the island's entire power grid. An estimated 2,975 excess deaths in the months following, mostly from healthcare system collapse. Recovery took years, exposing vulnerabilities of island infrastructure to Cat 4+ direct strikes.
