Formation Science

From Wind Shear to Funnel

Most violent tornadoes emerge from supercell thunderstorms — rotating, long-lived storms driven by a persistent mesocyclone. The process begins aloft, not at the ground.

When wind speed increases and direction shifts with altitude — known as wind shear — it creates horizontal rotation in the lower atmosphere, like an invisible rolling pin lying on its side. A powerful updraft in a developing supercell can tilt this horizontal rotation into the vertical, initiating a rotating updraft called a mesocyclone several kilometres wide.

As the mesocyclone intensifies, the lowering, rotating cloud base forms a wall cloud. Tornadogenesis — still not fully understood — occurs when air accelerating into the storm base wraps the rotation tightly into a narrow, intense column. The funnel cloud becomes visible as the vortex pressure drops enough to condense moisture. It becomes a tornado the instant the funnel touches the ground.

The lifecycle typically lasts minutes to an hour. Dissipation begins when the rear-flank downdraft wraps cool air completely around the base, cutting off the inflow of warm moist air that feeds the vortex.

Scale & Geography

EF Scale & Tornado Country

EF0–1

65–177 km/h. Broken branches, minor roof damage. Most tornadoes (80%) fall here.

EF2–3

178–266 km/h. Roofs torn off, cars overturned, trees uprooted. Significant injuries and fatalities.

EF4–5

267+ km/h. Well-built homes levelled, vehicles thrown, total destruction. Less than 2% of tornadoes.

Tornado Alley & Dixie Alley

The classic Tornado Alley (TX, OK, KS, NE) sees frequent spring activity. "Dixie Alley" (MS, AL, TN) produces more nocturnal and winter tornadoes — when people are asleep — making them disproportionately deadly.

Notable Events

History's Most Destructive Tornadoes

Joplin, Missouri — 2011

EF5. 1.6 km wide. 89 km path. 161 fatalities. Destroyed a hospital, flattened neighbourhoods and killed more people than any US tornado since 1947. Wind speeds estimated at 322+ km/h.

Moore, Oklahoma — 2013

EF5 struck Moore for the third time in 14 years. 2.1 km wide at peak. 24 fatalities including 7 children in an elementary school. Led to widespread storm shelter legislation in Oklahoma.

El Reno, Oklahoma — 2013

The widest tornado ever recorded: 4.2 km at peak width, larger than some cities. EF3 rating. Killed 3 veteran storm chasers when it unexpectedly shifted direction and expanded rapidly in seconds.
