Formation Science

How a Bolt is Born

Inside a towering cumulonimbus cloud, violent updrafts carry ice crystals and supercooled water droplets upward at speeds exceeding 160 km/h. Collisions between graupel (soft hail) and smaller ice crystals transfer charge: graupel acquires a negative charge and sinks toward the base; lighter crystals carry positive charge to the anvil top.

When the negative charge at the cloud base reaches a critical threshold (around 100 million volts), it launches an invisible stepped leader — a channel of ionized air that propagates downward in 50-metre steps at roughly 200,000 m/s, branching as it searches for a path to ground.

From tall objects below, positive streamers rise to meet the stepped leader. The instant they connect, a massive return stroke rockets upward at 100,000 km/s — about one-third the speed of light — releasing the stored energy in milliseconds. The rapid air expansion produces the shockwave we hear as thunder.

A single flash often contains 3–5 return strokes in rapid succession, each re-energizing the same channel — giving lightning its characteristic flicker.

Types & Curiosities

Beyond the Bolt

Cloud-to-Ground (CG)

Most dangerous. Negative charge bridges the cloud-ground gap. Only ~25% of all lightning but causes most damage.

Intracloud (IC) & Cloud-to-Cloud (CC)

The majority (~75%) stays within or between clouds. Creates the diffuse "sheet lightning" glow visible on distant storms.

St. Elmo's Fire

A continuous corona discharge from pointed objects during strong electric fields — glowing plasma seen on ship masts and aircraft wings.

Ball Lightning

Rare, unexplained luminous orbs reported after strikes. Typically 10–50 cm in diameter, lasting seconds to minutes. No consensus on mechanism.

Impact & Safety

Infrastructure, Wildfire & the 30/30 Rule

Infrastructure

Lightning causes over $1 billion in property damage annually in the US alone. Power surges triggered by nearby strikes destroy electronics; direct hits ignite structures and can vaporize water in masonry, causing explosive spalling.

Wildfires

Lightning ignites an estimated 10,000 wildfires per year in the US. "Dry lightning" — strikes from high-based storms with little rain reaching the ground — is especially destructive in the American West and Australia.

The 30/30 Rule

If you count fewer than 30 seconds between flash and thunder, lightning is within 10 km — seek shelter immediately. Wait 30 minutes after the last flash before going back outside. Aviation uses lightning detectors to reroute flights and protect ground crews.
